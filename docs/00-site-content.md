# Part 1. Instructions for the agent

## Context

The site already exists in the `portfolio-lamain` folder. It is plain HTML and CSS, with no framework.

- `site/index.html` is the home page.
- `site/projects/*.html` are the project pages. They are generated by `site/build_projects.py`. Update the content there and rebuild with `python3 build_projects.py`, or replace the generator with static pages if that is simpler.
- `site/assets/css/style.css` holds the whole visual system. Do not change the art direction.

## Art direction to keep

The site is designed like a set of technical drawings. The background is off-white paper with small crosses every 28 px. There is a single accent colour, blue `#1A0DAB`, used for links, labels and the hand drawing. Body text uses Times New Roman at 20 px. Project names are italic serif, drawn as outlines in blue with `-webkit-text-stroke`. Small labels are monospace, uppercase and blue. Dark mode follows the system setting. Animations stop when the visitor prefers reduced motion. The hero shows the line drawing of the hand, with a slow highlight sweeping from top to bottom and three annotations that appear one at a time.

## Writing rules

These rules apply to every visible piece of text on the site.

- No colons in sentences.
- No em dashes or en dashes. Use full stops or commas instead.
- Short, complete sentences in plain English.
- Keep the texts below exactly as written. Do not rephrase them or add adjectives.

## Placeholders

Values in double braces are not known yet. Keep them as they are, so the owner can find and fill them later with a search for `{{`.

`{{EMAIL}}`, `{{GITHUB_URL}}`, `{{LINKEDIN_URL}}`, `{{CV_PATH}}` (default `cv.pdf`), `{{LAMAIN_GITHUB_URL}}`, `{{LEPOTAGER_VIDEO_URL}}`, `{{LEPOTAGER_CODE_URL}}`, `{{ETTC_PAPER_TITLE}}`, `{{ETTC_PAPER_URL}}`, `{{INSA_PAPER_TITLE}}`, `{{INSA_PAPER_YEAR}}`, `{{INSA_PAPER_URL}}`, `{{UPDATED_DATE}}`.

# LaMain

An open-source robot hand with touch, light enough to go on the SO-101 arm.

## Why a hand

Language models became good because the internet was full of text to learn from. Robots that manipulate objects have nothing like that. There is no large pile of data showing hands grasping, pressing and adjusting their grip.

Today most of this data comes from two sources. The first is egocentric video, where people film themselves doing tasks. It is cheap to collect, but a camera cannot feel anything, and the fingers are often hidden behind the object. Some companies even pay people to film themselves at scale, which raises its own ethical questions. The second source is teleoperation, where a person drives the robot directly. The data is better because it records what the motors actually do, but it is slow and expensive, so it does not scale.

Touch makes the problem harder. It is not a commodity. A lab that wants to study dexterous tasks with touch usually buys an expensive robot hand, adds tactile sensors to it, and mounts it on an industrial arm that costs around €20,000. Very few people can do that. This is also why many tactile benchmarks do not use hands at all.

## Why the SO-101

The SO-101 is the arm of the LeRobot project from Hugging Face. It is one of the most widely used open-source robot arms, and it is how many students and engineers learn robot learning today. Schools buy it by the dozen.

If this arm had a real hand with touch, anyone could start recording tactile manipulation data. That data could then feed open models for dexterous manipulation. This is the idea behind LaMain.

## What already exists

Pollen Robotics designed the Amazing Hand, an open-source hand that inspired a lot of this project. It weighs about 400 grams and has no touch sensors. The SO-101 is a small arm that cannot carry much, so with this hand there is almost nothing left to lift.

The Barrett Hand also has three fingers, but it is not shaped like a human hand. We wanted an anthropomorphic hand on purpose. A hand shaped like ours can be teleoperated from a human hand, and the data it records can be linked with egocentric video of people doing the same task.

## The goals

We set three targets. The hand must weigh less than 200 grams, so the arm keeps most of its payload for the objects it picks up. It must cost less than €200. And it must carry tactile sensors. It also has to follow the conventions of the LeRobot library, so the community can pick it up easily.

## The design

LaMain has three fingers and is underactuated. It uses five servos for five degrees of freedom. One servo bends the index finger, one bends the middle finger, one spreads both of them apart, and two servos in series move the thumb.

We started from the architecture of the Amazing Hand and reused its parts where we could. Pollen did real engineering work on it, and reusing parts keeps the cost down. But there is one big difference. The Amazing Hand drives each finger with two small servos. We use one servo per finger to save weight, and a single small servo does not have enough torque to hold an object. So we moved to larger Feetech servos.

This is the main trade-off of the whole project. Small servos are light, so the arm can still lift things, but the hand cannot grip. Large servos grip well, but then the hand is too heavy for the arm. We are trying to find the point where both work.

Spreading the two fingers apart with a single servo also needed a new mechanism. Julien Navet, the mechanical engineer I work with, designed it.

## Touch

The first tactile sensor will be FlexiTac, a flexible piezoresistive sensor. It changes resistance when it is pressed. Later we want to compare it with other tactile skins.

## Software and data

My part of the project is integration. I am adding LaMain to the LeRobot library as a new robot, with a dataset format that records the servo positions and the tactile signals together. Imitation learning policies will then be able to learn from both.

Once the hand works, I will record a first large dataset and compare the hand with the standard SO-101 gripper on the same tasks.

## Learning on the hand

The hand is a means, not the goal. The goal is to learn manipulation with touch.

I will start with ACT, the imitation learning model I used at the LeRobot hackathon, as a first baseline. Then I plan to fine-tune a vision-language-action model, or VLA, on the data recorded with the hand. These models take camera images and an instruction in plain language, and they output the robot's actions. LeRobot already supports several of them, such as SmolVLA and π0.

Recent research also adds touch as an input to these models. The first question I want to answer with LaMain is simple. On the same tasks, does the same policy do better with touch than without it?

## Where it stands

Nothing has been tested yet. The parts are ordered, and the first assembly comes next. The weight and cost above are targets, not measurements. I will assemble the hand, report the problems to Julien, and we will iterate.

The goal is a hand that can grasp objects by the end of November 2026. Then we can start recording data.

I will document every step on this page and on GitHub, with photos of the parts and of the assembled hand.

LaMain is designed with Julien Navet, who does the mechanical design. I handle the assembly, the tests, the LeRobot integration and the datasets.

The bill of materials and the design files are on [GitHub]({{LAMAIN_GITHUB_URL}}).



# LePotager

A robot arm that sorts fruit by touch, built in 48 hours.

## The hackathon

In June 2026 I took part in a robotics hackathon in Copenhagen, built around LeRobot. The theme was a mini-factory, and we had 48 hours to build one. I worked with Doga, Akan and José Vasquez.

We did not want to build a demo for the sake of it. We wanted to take a real problem and solve it in a smart way.

## The problem

In fruit processing plants, the most common way to check a fruit is a camera that looks at its colour. This wastes a lot of food. Some fruit looks dull but is perfectly good. Other fruit looks great but is soft inside. Colour does not tell you how firm a fruit is. Touch does.

## What we built

Doga is doing his PhD on a low-cost, open-source pressure sensor made of Velostat, a material whose resistance changes when it is pressed. We put this sensor on the tip of the SO-101 gripper.

The pipeline works like this. Fruits are dropped in a zone. The arm picks one up and places it in a test zone. It presses the fruit three times to measure how firm it is. Then it sorts the fruit according to its firmness.

The sensor is experimental and sometimes fails, so getting a clean signal took work. It is read by an ESP32 microcontroller, which sends the values to the computer over a serial link. We then write them into the dataset, next to the camera images.

The policy is ACT, an imitation learning model available in LeRobot. We trained it on 50 demonstrations, each with camera images and tactile data.

## Why the cameras matter

With this kind of model, the view must stay exactly the same between training and use. If a camera moves even a little, the background changes and the policy can fail completely.

We used two cameras. One sits on the wrist and moves with the gripper. The other is fixed above the scene and sees everything. It is a RealSense depth camera, and it has to stay perfectly still.

## What went wrong

The first problem came right at the start. The servo IDs were not set correctly, so we had to reconfigure them by hand before we could even teleoperate the arm.

The second problem was the overhead camera. We had fixed it on top of delivery boxes with tape. We collected our data, trained the policy, and everything worked. The next morning the camera had fallen, pulled down by its own weight. The view was different, so the whole dataset was useless. We had to record everything again.

The third problem was more interesting. I had read a paper showing that demonstrations of error recovery make a policy more robust. The idea is to also show the robot how to correct itself after a mistake. So we added many recovery demonstrations. We added too many. In the data, recovering from a mistake became more common than grasping the fruit correctly. The robot learned that behaviour. It kept moving next to the fruit and then coming back, every single time.

This taught me something simple. A policy does what the data shows most often. The balance of the demonstrations matters as much as their quality.

## The result

The system worked live, from picking to sorting, and we won the Most Creative Project award. Most other teams built demonstrations, such as sorting red cubes or driving a small car. We started from a real problem and thought about it as a product.

This project is also where LaMain comes from. A single sensor on a gripper could already tell a soft fruit from a firm one. A hand with touch on every finger could do much more.

# DagoBERT

Programming flight-test hardware in plain English, at Safran Data Systems.

## The context

I spent three years as an R&D apprentice in the innovation team of Safran Data Systems. The company makes flight-test instrumentation, the equipment that records data during the test flights of new aircraft.

One of its products is a modular acquisition system called XMA. One of its modules, the OBP, is a small on-board computer. It can convert raw sensor values into physical units, run frequency analyses, or keep only the important data before it is sent to the ground.

One example shows why this matters. When engineers study how a wing vibrates, the wing can be damaged if it stays too long at its resonant frequency. Usually the data is sent to the ground, analysed there, and then the pilot is told to slow down. That takes time. If the analysis runs on board, the alarm can reach the pilot much faster.

## The problem

The OBP is powerful, but it has to be programmed in C or C++. Most flight-test engineers do not write C. So a very useful tool stays out of reach for the people who need it.

My project was a proof of concept to change that. The idea was to let engineers describe what they want in plain English, and to turn that description into a program for the OBP. I called it DagoBERT.

## The constraints

Three constraints shaped the design.

The first is confidentiality. Test data and programs are sensitive, so a cloud service like ChatGPT was not an option. The language model had to run locally, on a company machine. This limited us to models of about 8 billion parameters.

The second is reliability. A language model is never fully predictable. A small change in the request can change the answer. In flight testing, a random bug is not acceptable. So I decided that the model would only understand the request. It would never write the code itself.

The third is control. The engineer must be able to see what will run on the aircraft, and change it if needed.

## How it works

The chain has three parts.

First, the engineer types a request, for example "take one input, compute the average over five values, and send the result to an output". A local language model turns this sentence into a structured file that lists the processing blocks and how they are connected. To make this reliable, the model receives a prompt with a few examples of requests and correct answers before each question.

Second, the blocks open in GNU Radio, an open-source tool that shows processing chains as connected blocks. The engineer can check them and edit them. If the model got the request 90% right, the engineer fixes the last 10% by hand instead of asking again.

Third, a Python script turns the blocks into C++ code. To keep this generation simple and safe, I wrote a small C++ library where each type of block is a class. An input, an average or a maximum each has its own class, and each block passes its results to the next one. The script also writes the configuration file that describes the inputs and outputs. The code is then cross-compiled for the ARM processor of the OBP and loaded with Safran's configuration software.

I also built a simple desktop interface that brings the three parts together, with a log window to find errors quickly.

## The results

The complete chain works, from a sentence to a program running on the OBP.

The choice of model made a big difference. A small model gave the right answer only 30 to 40% of the time and took about a minute per request on an office computer. A Llama 3 model on a more powerful workstation reached 90 to 95% with a better prompt, and answered in 3 to 5 seconds.

This work led to a paper that I co-authored at ETTC 2025, the European Test and Telemetry Conference.

## What I learned

At the start, I chose Blockly, a block library written in JavaScript. I spent days learning a language that added nothing to the project. GNU Radio was already based on blocks and written in Python, like the rest of the project. Switching saved the proof of concept.

The main lesson is to build the simplest chain that works from start to finish first, even with hard-coded values and no interface. Then you improve it one piece at a time. I also learned to ask for help sooner instead of staying stuck for days.

When an intern joined the project, I split it into three independent parts so he could work on one without knowing the others. With hindsight, the task I gave him was too big for two weeks, and I would plan it differently today.

## Limits

DagoBERT is a proof of concept. It supports only a few blocks and covers one or two of the main uses of the OBP. The code generation should be made more robust, and the tool needs to be tested with real users.

During the same apprenticeship I also designed FPGA logic for real-time current and voltage acquisition on flight-test equipment.

# Capra Robotics

Three problems on real autonomous mobile robots. Two of them are about computer vision models, one is about localisation.

## The context

I spent six months at Capra Robotics in Aarhus, Denmark, a company of about 45 people that builds autonomous mobile robots for indoor and outdoor logistics. Their software runs on ROS 2, with Zenoh for communication, inside Docker containers.

I worked the same way on all three missions. First I built the simplest chain that worked on the robot, from end to end. Then I improved one piece at a time.


## 1. Seeing what the LiDAR cannot see

### The problem

The robots navigate mainly with LiDAR. A LiDAR is good at detecting walls and large obstacles, but it misses low ones like a plank on the floor or a kerb. It also makes the opposite mistake. It can treat a ramp or tall grass as a wall.

The idea was to add a depth camera and a segmentation model. The model labels every pixel of the image as ground or obstacle. The obstacle pixels are then combined with the depth information and added to the robot's navigation map, so the robot avoids them.

### A first version

The first version ran in Python on the robot's main computer, at less than one image per second. That was slow, but it was enough to prove the idea. The robot stopped in front of a plank that its LiDAR could not see.

### Building the dataset

Generic models did not work well on the robot's images, so I needed my own dataset. Labelling images by hand is slow, so I used the models to help.

I labelled 183 images by hand, including difficult cases such as stairs and door sills. I trained a large model on them and used it to label the next batch automatically. I corrected its mistakes, trained again, and repeated the process. In the end the dataset had 1,840 images, with about ten times less manual labelling than doing it all by hand.

### Choosing the model

I compared four families of segmentation models on the same data. SegFormer gave the best results.

The most interesting result was about data. With the larger dataset, the smallest SegFormer improved more than it would have by switching to a model 17 times bigger. More data beat a bigger model.

I also changed the training loss. The first masks had holes in the ground areas, so I added a term to the loss that measures how well the shapes overlap. This removed most of the holes.

The final model is SegFormer B0, running at the camera's native resolution of 848 by 480 pixels. It reaches an IoU of 0.981 on 367 images it never saw during training.

### What I did not finish

The target was 30 images per second on a Jetson Orin Nano. The model runs at 5.7. I profiled the pipeline with NVIDIA Nsight to understand why. Most of the time was lost outside the model itself, in Python overhead, in the processor waiting for the graphics chip, in memory copies, and in steps that ran one after the other instead of in parallel. I estimated the fixes, such as TensorRT and a C++ pipeline, but did not implement them. For the robot, accuracy mattered more than speed at that stage.

There is also a deeper limit. Segmentation says what a surface is, not whether the robot can drive on it. A gentle slope and a steep slope are both ground. The next step is to combine the segmentation with an elevation map built from the LiDAR. I ported that software to our version of ROS 2 before the priorities moved to anonymisation.


## 2. Anonymising images inside the camera

### The problem

ARLI is a robot that drives along city sidewalks and scans them to keep a digital model of the city up to date. The project is funded by the European Space Agency, with the Technical University of Denmark and the City of Aarhus.

The robot needs a front camera, so it films people and cars. European data protection law requires faces and number plates to be blurred. The earlier the blurring happens, the less there is to protect.

The usual setup with this camera is that the camera detects faces and the robot's computer blurs them. That means clear images still leave the camera. The goal was to do everything inside the camera, so that no identifiable image ever leaves it.

### The constraints

The camera is an OAK-D. Its vision chip can only run a limited set of operations. It has 512 megabytes of memory, and its two small processors have no vector instructions. The model also had to allow commercial use, which ruled out the recent versions of YOLO under the AGPL licence.

### How it works

I fine-tuned YOLOX-S, a detection model under a permissive licence, to detect faces and number plates. The training data combined public datasets with 920 images from the robot, labelled automatically. The two classes were not equally represented in the data, so I weighted the training loss to balance them.

I then converted the model step by step, from PyTorch to ONNX to OpenVINO, and finally to a 16-bit format that the camera's chip can run. Detection runs on the vision chip. Blurring and image compression run on the small processors. Only the blurred video stream leaves the camera.

### What went wrong

The first working version had a delay of 2.5 seconds, and the delay kept growing. The model was not the cause. The images were waiting in memory queues between the processing steps. When I made the queues shorter, the pipeline started to run out of images instead. The solution was queues that hold a single image and replace it when a new one arrives, instead of waiting.

A second problem appeared after that. Blurred images compress badly, so each image became about five times bigger. When the network could not send them fast enough, the whole pipeline froze. Now the camera drops images when the network is too slow, and the delay stays stable.

### The result

The delay went from 2.5 seconds to 345 milliseconds and stays stable. The model reaches a detection score of 0.85 (mAP at 0.5). The pipeline runs on ARLI in real conditions.


## 3. Docking to a food cart

### The problem

In food factories, workers push heavy steel carts by hand, often 250 to 350 kilograms, over 100 to 300 metres, on wet and cold floors. Capra's robot can tow these carts with a trailer. Dropping a cart off was already automated. Picking one up was not, and that is what customers wanted.

A worker pushes the cart into a zone by hand, so its real position is often 20 centimetres and 15 degrees away from what the map says. To lock onto the cart, the trailer needs a precision of about 20 millimetres. The trailer is behind the robot, so the robot has to approach in reverse, and it must keep track of the cart during the whole manoeuvre.

### The constraints

The robot's computer has no graphics chip, so everything runs on the processor. Each LiDAR point has an error of 1 to 3.5 centimetres, which is more than the tolerance. The carts are made of polished stainless steel, so some parts of them simply do not appear in the LiDAR data. And below about 50 centimetres the sensor saturates and distorts the points, exactly where precision matters most.

### The approach

The method belongs to Capra, so I will not describe it. The main ideas are that no single measurement is trusted, that the estimate becomes more precise as the robot gets closer, and that the system works without adding markers to the customers' carts.

### The result

The robot picked up the cart 18 times out of 18 during an hour of continuous tests. Each update takes less than 45 milliseconds on the processor. This unlocks the fully automatic mode. The next steps are difficult cases, such as carts partly hidden by their neighbours, and a longer test campaign on a customer site.
